Bottom line
Run the full-product cohort (Path C) as the proof engine for a B2B2C licence or sale to a broadcaster (Path D). Not consumer launch, not a marketplace listing — both of those price the asset badly. This one doesn't.

The B2B2C reframe is the single most valuable idea in this document, and it's yours. Against a broadcaster's build-vs-buy decision the asset compares to a $900k–2.5M custom build, not to other codebases on Flippa. It also inverts the two weaknesses that sink every other path: you stop needing distribution (they have the audience) and you stop needing a gambling licence (they carry it, or it ships free-to-play — exactly as your no-cash-out design already assumes).

The cohort still matters, because a broadcaster needs a clean engagement metric — but crucially not scale. 300 players can produce that. And the clock is real: the three seasons only overlap until December.

The three findings that drive this

Finding 1 — Your floor valuation is self-defeating

VALUATION_ANALYSIS.md sets a £200k–£450k "cost-to-recreate" floor, derived from 2–3 engineers × 11–16 months at £18–28k/month. But your own git history says this was built by one non-technical founder with AI assistance in roughly four months — 1,217 commits since 2026-04-18.

Those two facts cannot both price the asset. And §11.1 of the buyer-facing brief offers the build history to buyers on request — handing a sophisticated acquirer the exact evidence that destroys the floor. Cost-to-recreate is only a valid floor when recreating is genuinely expensive. The same technology that let you build this collapsed the cost of rebuilding it, for the buyer as much as for you.

Finding 2 — In every sport you built, the incumbent is free and official

Fantasy Premier League: 11.43M verified teams, free, run by the league itself. F1 Fantasy: 10M+ registered players, free, run by F1. These aren't competitors in the ordinary sense — they're rights-holders giving the core loop away as fan engagement, funded by broadcast and sponsorship money. You cannot out-price free, and you cannot out-license the league.

Your differentiation (private multi-sport circles, social layer, coin economy) is real, but it's a layer on top of a commodity core — and it's precisely the layer Sleeper is building with $217M of raised capital and 4M+ users.

Finding 3 — The consumer asset market has essentially no bid

Acquire.com filters pre-revenue submissions out at review. Flippa will accept the listing but its buyers price against businesses with real traction and lowball code-only assets accordingly. There is no liquid market at £450–750k for a pre-revenue consumer codebase.

Any such buyer's diligence also finds what your own August audit found: 75 of 271 migrations fail a from-scratch replay, thin money-logic test coverage, no DR or staging, Supabase lock-in, unconfirmed data-feed transferability.

This finding is confined to the D2C frame. See Path D — against a broadcaster's build-vs-buy comparison the same asset is priced entirely differently, and the internal valuation becomes defensible.

Finding 4 — You have more demand proof than the documents claim

I said "zero demand proof" in the first version of this analysis. That was wrong, and the correction comes from querying the pilot data directly rather than reading the docs.

In the World Cup pilot (11 Jun – 19 Jul 2026), across the two leagues where betting was switched on: 14 of 16 members placed bets — 87.5% adoption. 250 submissions across 39 bets. 17.9 bets per participant, one user at 34. Average 4.6 active weeks out of ~5.5; 9 of 14 (64%) active in four or more distinct weeks.

That is a real behavioural signal on the exact mechanic you believe is the differentiator, and it is a far better opening line than "pre-revenue." Caveats are heavy and must be stated: n=16, almost certainly friends and family, and it ran during a World Cup — the single highest-engagement event in sport. But heavily-caveated real engagement beats no engagement, and it reframes the whole conversation.

Your three-part USP, scored against the evidence

The stated differentiator is the combination: (i) multi-sport in one room with the same people, (ii) a social layer aimed at casual fans rather than die-hard fantasy players, (iii) a fast, direct betting layer. I think that combination is genuinely coherent and it's what makes the product non-generic. But the three legs are not equally proven, and the imbalance should drive priorities.

Your most proven leg is the betting layer. Your least proven leg is multi-sport — which is the one the entire architecture, the three sport modules, and the strategic pitch all rest on. Every claim about "the same group across sports" is currently an assertion.

That's not an argument against the thesis. It's an argument that the cohort's primary job is to test leg (i), because legs (ii) and (iii) already have at least a signal and leg (i) has literally none.

A structural finding hiding in the pilot data

Only 2 leagues had any bets at all, and all 39 bet instances were commissioner-created. The betting layer — your best-performing feature — has a supply dependency: no commissioner, no bets, no engagement. That is a serious product risk at any scale beyond a handful of leagues.

p2p_challenges (user-generated, peer-to-peer) is precisely the fix for this, and it has zero rows. So the single highest-value validation task in the entire plan is proving that users create bets for each other without a commissioner driving it. Not a footnote — the top priority.

The economics, honestly

At a sports-category CPI of ~$26.81 and a 2–5% conversion rate, a paid-acquisition strategy implies a cost per paying user in the hundreds of dollars. One industry estimate puts North American fantasy user acquisition at $300–350 per user given category marketing intensity. Against coin-pack revenue of a few pounds per user, paid acquisition is not viable at any scale you can fund.

That leaves organic: communities, word-of-mouth, creator partnerships, SEO. All are viable, all are slow, and all require sustained content and community work — which is a full-time job that is not engineering. This is the crux: the remaining work in Path A is almost entirely work you haven't been doing and haven't demonstrated.

Regulatory — treat this as a hard gate, not a footnote

The UK Gambling Commission explicitly lists "fantasy football-type competitions" under pool betting. Its guidance on virtual currencies states that where facilities for gambling are offered using such items, a licence is required in the same manner as for casino chips that can later be exchanged for cash.

Your no-cash-out design is a genuine and well-reasoned mitigation — it's the difference between a virtual-goods model and gambling. But you have users paying real money via Stripe for coins that are then staked on outcomes in P2P challenges. Whether that constitutes a "prize of money's worth" is a question for a gambling solicitor, not for a founder's reading of the guidance. Get a written opinion before you take a single pound of coin revenue. This is also the item most likely to blow up a sale later if left unresolved.

Verdict on Path A

Not viable as a broad public launch as currently resourced. The product is ready enough; the business around it doesn't exist. You'd be entering the highest-CPI app category, against free official incumbents, with no distribution, no marketing budget, no ops team, and an unresolved regulatory question, as one person.

But the failure mode is audience scale, not product scope. Cutting features to "launch smaller" is the wrong economy — it strips out the multi-sport container that is the only reason to choose this over FPL, and leaves you testing a commodity. Cut the audience instead: full product, 200–400 invited players, a size one person can operate. That's Path C, and it's a genuine version of Path A rather than a retreat from it.

What a buyer's diligence will find

Your own documents are unusually candid, which is a real asset in a sale — but it means the findings are already written down. A buyer will read:

  • Schema irreproducibility (DATA-1). 75 of 271 migrations fail a from-scratch replay. A fix was applied directly to production and never captured as a migration. The repo cannot rebuild the database it runs on. Mitigated by supabase/schema.sql, but the audit trail gap is the finding.
  • Test coverage. 56 unit tests and 84 E2E UI tests is meaningful progress — but coverage of the money and scoring paths is thin relative to the risk those paths carry.
  • No DR (OPS-1). Single environment, no point-in-time recovery, manual backups.
  • Supabase lock-in. "Run it on our cloud" is a re-platforming project, not a config change.
  • Data-feed transferability unconfirmed. Forza, OpenF1, RapidAPI commercial terms on change of control — still an open question in your own brief.
  • Three headline features never exercised in production. p2p_challenges, trophy_ledger, player_boxes all have zero rows. "Shipped" means merged, not validated.
  • Zero demand proof. ~75 registered users, 34 in nothing, £0 revenue, site behind a maintenance wall.
The candour trade-off

Your documentation quality genuinely raises value — it removes onboarding risk and signals integrity, and buyers reward that. But be deliberate about sequencing: don't volunteer the internal valuation reasoning or the raw build history (§11.1) until you understand what the buyer's own alternative costs. Full transparency on technical state, yes. Volunteering the argument that defeats your own floor, no.

Realistic outcome if you run a sale process now

Months of outbound to a thin buyer list, with the most likely outcomes being no bid, or a bid in the £25k–£90k range from a single strategic buyer with no competitive tension. A code-marketplace listing would clear faster at £8k–£30k. Weigh that against roughly four months of full-time work and whatever the platform cost to run.

Verdict on Path B

This class has a live, dated precedent that is almost exactly your proposed transaction. Free-to-play, in-game coins, division-based leaderboards, ad-free.

Why it fits you specifically: your no-cash-out virtual coin economy is the exact compliance shape an operator wants for a top-of-funnel product — engagement without a licensable event. Nobody puts this class on the list, and it may be the best first close. A brand sponsoring WWC 2027 (or any tournament) needs an activation. You have an activation with a measured four-week retention number attached. The budget comes from marketing, the decision cycle is weeks, the technical diligence is light, and success is measured in engagement — the one thing you can already evidence. Smallest cheque of the six, but it is revenue, it is a logo, and it converts you from "pre-revenue asset" to "vendor with a client."

F · Leagues, federations and clubs Weakest fit

Single-sport bodies structurally cannot use leg (i), which is your headline differentiator — a tennis federation has no interest in an F1 paddock. Only worth pursuing for genuinely multi-sport bodies or multi-club groups. Long cycles, and you'd be selling the least distinctive version of the product.

What this path demands that the D2C path doesn't

This reverses my earlier "defer DATA-1 / OPS-1" advice

In the D2C frame those were deferrable — you'd only clear them once a buyer appeared. In the B2B2C frame they are entry requirements. No broadcaster's technical diligence accepts "we can't rebuild the schema from the repo," "there's no staging," or "there's no point-in-time recovery." A consumer buyer might shrug; a procurement process will not.

  • Multi-tenancy and white-label theming. Currently single-tenant. This is real engineering, not configuration, and it's the biggest genuine gap.
  • Data-feed agnosticism. They bring their own rights and feeds. The provider-adapter seam and the Opta stub are exactly the right groundwork — finish and demo them.
  • Integration surface. SSO into their identity system, an embeddable/SDK path rather than a standalone app, and their design system.
  • DATA-1, OPS-1, TEST-1 cleared. Schema reproducibility, staging + PITR, money-logic test coverage. Non-negotiable for procurement.
  • Solo-vendor risk. The hardest objection, and it has no clean answer. Mitigate with source-code escrow, a licence-plus-support structure, or by selling the IP outright rather than becoming a dependency.

Verdict on Path D

This is the most valuable path available, and it's where the internal valuation actually lives. It inverts the two weaknesses that sink the other paths: you stop needing distribution (they have the audience) and you stop needing a gambling licence (they carry it, or it ships free-to-play). It also matches what you can realistically resource — one strong pitch to one broadcaster, not a consumer growth machine.

The costs are real: 6–18 month sales cycles, procurement-grade diligence, and multi-tenancy work that doesn't yet exist. But the proof it requires is a clean engagement metric, not scale — and a 300-person cohort can produce that. Which is why Path C doesn't compete with this. It feeds it.

Path C — Full product, controlled audience

The move
Launch the entire thesis — clubhouse, all three sports live simultaneously, P2P challenges, trophies — to a deliberately invited cohort of 200–400. Instrument it. Run 10–12 weeks. Then decide with data.

Full product scope is non-negotiable: a mono-sport launch tests the commodity layer, and the commodity layer is the part you lose to the free official incumbent anyway. But audience scope is a separate decision from product scope, and going wide is the worse bet — three of your four differentiators have never been touched by a human.

Why not open the doors completely

p2p_challenges = 0 rows. trophy_ledger = 0 rows. player_boxes = 0 rows. Merged is not validated. Your live-ops history already includes manual scoring corrections (13 managers, retroactive clean-sheets) at ~50 users, run by one person. An invited cohort isn't timidity — it's the largest audience you can actually support while three headline features meet their first real user.

Why this beats committing to either path now

  • It tests the real hypothesis. Not "is our fantasy game good" — that question's answer doesn't matter. The question is whether a multi-sport social container is something people want. Only a full-scope launch can answer it.
  • It's the same work either way. Engagement data is the primary input to a launch decision and the primary missing input to a sale price. Nothing else has that property.
  • It's falsifiable in both directions. If the cross-sport rate is low, you've proven your own "then I should close shop" hypothesis with evidence in 12 weeks rather than assuming it, or discovering it in 12 months.
  • It de-risks the sale regardless. Exercising the three never-used surfaces converts "merged" into "validated" — which is a diligence answer whichever way the numbers land.
The calendar is the constraint — this is time-critical

As of 21 Aug 2026: F1 has 11 races left, ending early December. European football 2026/27 seasons are kicking off right now. Tennis has 5 genuinely future tournaments.

This is the one window where all three run at once — and a multi-sport clubhouse where only one sport has live content isn't demonstrable at all. The cross-sport trophy ledger has nothing to aggregate. The thesis is only testable while the seasons overlap, and that overlap closes when F1 ends in December. Football registration is a now-action, not a later-action.

The metric this whole launch exists to produce

Cross-sport participation rate

Of active users, what percentage compete in two or more sports? That single number is the thesis. If it's high, the clubhouse container is real and you are not another fantasy app. If it's low, the container adds nothing — and by your own logic, that's the answer.

Retention is secondary. Everything else — screens, formats, polish — is in service of this one measurement. Instrument for it specifically, not just for generic DAU.

Pre-commit to these decision criteria

Agree these now, while you're not yet invested in a particular answer. Four signals, in priority order:

Cross-sport rate >35% + week-8 retention >40% + organic invites spreading Thesis proven Commit to Path A. Raise or bootstrap. You now hold the only thing investors and acquirers actually price.
Cross-sport rate >35% but retention 20–40%, no organic spread Thesis right, distribution wrong Strongest possible sale story: "the multi-sport hook works, we lack reach." This is the £120k–£400k conversation, not £25k–£90k.

What makes this the right call

Every branch leaves you better off than deciding today. The upside branch gives you a fundable business. The middle branch multiplies the achievable sale price by more than the sprint costs. The downside branch tells you to stop — which, four months in, is genuinely valuable and far cheaper to learn now than after another six months of building.

What NOT to do

  • Don't build new features. The problem is not missing capability. Every hour spent on a new format is an hour not spent on the calendar window, which closes in December.
  • Do start DATA-1 / OPS-1 — revised. I originally said defer these. That holds only if the buyer is a consumer acquirer. If you are pursuing Path D they are entry requirements for procurement, not optional hardening, and they have long lead times. Start them in parallel with the cohort.
  • Don't measure only your own sport. Leg (i) of the USP has zero evidence. If the cohort ends with everyone in one sport, you've learned nothing about the thesis regardless of how good retention looks.
  • Don't take coin revenue before the legal opinion lands. Small revenue is not worth the regulatory exposure or the risk of derailing a later sale. Run the cohort on granted coins until the opinion is in.
  • Don't open signup publicly. Invited cohorts only. You need a surface you can support solo while three headline features meet their first real user.
  • Don't measure only retention. A high-retention single-sport cohort would look like success and would actually be the failure case. Cross-sport rate is the number.

The honest risk in this plan

The binding constraint is recruitment, not engineering. Every technical step above is tractable and largely sequenced already by your own August audit. Getting 200–400 people into 20–40 clubhouses with no budget, no distribution and no existing audience is the part with no runbook and no prior evidence that you can do it.

If, four weeks into recruiting, you have 30 people rather than 300, that is itself the answer — and it arrives before most of the money is spent. Treat the recruitment number as an early checkpoint, not something to discover at week 12.

The parallel track is partly a hedge against exactly this. A broadcaster doesn't need you to have an audience — they have one. Under Path D a small cohort that produces clean cross-sport and betting engagement data is worth more than a large one that produces noise. That reframes a recruitment shortfall from fatal to survivable, but only if the outreach has already been running for months by the time you find out.
